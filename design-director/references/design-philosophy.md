# Design Philosophy

Principles for making judgment calls. These are not rules — they're beliefs.
Use them when you're at a decision fork and need to orient.

---

## CORE BELIEFS

### 1. Every default is an abdication

When you accept a framework's default styling, a template's placeholder font, or a library's
out-of-the-box color scheme, you haven't saved time — you've borrowed visual mediocrity.
Every design default was designed for the average case, which means it's wrong for every
specific case, including yours.

*In practice:* Before using any default, ask: "Did I choose this, or did I not choose it?"
If the latter, decide. Even deciding to keep the default is better than not deciding.

---

### 2. Restraint is the hardest and most important skill

The easiest move in design is to add. Another color. Another element. More emphasis.
The hardest move — and the one that separates professional work — is to trust that less is enough.

Every element you add competes for attention. Every color you add dilutes your accent.
Every special treatment you add devalues the others.

*In practice:* When you want to add something, ask: "What would I remove to make room for this?"
If the answer is "nothing," then the addition is probably wrong.

---

### 3. Typography does most of the work

In most visual outputs, 70–80% of what the viewer experiences is typography. The typeface
sets the personality. The scale sets the hierarchy. The spacing sets the rhythm.
Color, images, and decoration do the remaining 20–30%.

A beautifully typeset piece with no color will beat a colorful piece with poor typography.
Every time.

*In practice:* Spend disproportionate time on typeface selection and size scale.
The remaining design decisions will be easier once typography is right.

---

### 4. Space is not emptiness — it's material

Designers call it "negative space," but it's not negative. It's active.
Whitespace groups things. Separates things. Signals importance. Creates rhythm.
Communicates premium positioning.

Products that feel expensive use more space. Documents that feel authoritative use generous margins.
Dashboards that feel clear have data that breathes.

*In practice:* Your first spacing instinct is too tight. Double it. Then ask if it needs more.

---

### 5. The details make it — or break it

Two layouts can have the same structure, the same typeface, the same colors, and one looks
professional and the other looks amateur. The difference is almost always in the details:

- The border-radius that's slightly inconsistent
- The shadow that doesn't match the light source
- The icon that came from a different set
- The number that's using proportional instead of tabular figures
- The 2px misalignment between elements on the same grid

*In practice:* After completing the major design decisions, spend time auditing the small things.
The quality of the details is what viewers remember, even if they can't articulate why.

---

### 6. Hierarchy before decoration

No amount of visual sophistication compensates for unclear hierarchy.
If the viewer can't tell what to look at first, second, and third — the piece has failed.

Hierarchy is established through: size, weight, contrast, position, space, and color — in roughly
that order of reliability. Color hierarchy is the weakest; size hierarchy is the strongest.

*In practice:* Test hierarchy before finalizing anything. Can you tell, at a glance, what's most important?
If you need to think about it, the hierarchy isn't clear enough.

---

### 7. Design for the best possible reader

Don't design for the distracted, hurried, or disengaged reader. Design for the reader who
deserves your best work — the person who will give it full attention and notice the quality.

Designing down to the lowest engagement level produces work that no one loves.
Designing up produces work that rewards attention.

*In practice:* Make choices that a thoughtful viewer would notice and appreciate,
even if most viewers won't consciously register them.

---

### 8. Consistency enables creativity

A design system — even a simple one — creates the constraints that make bold choices possible.
When typeface, color, spacing, and component behavior are consistent, you can make one element
dramatically different and have it read as intentional, not as an error.

Without consistency, dramatic choices just look like mistakes.

*In practice:* Establish the system first (color palette, type scale, spacing scale, component rules).
Then make your bold moves within it.

---

## ON BALANCING EXPERTISE WITH TASTE

Design excellence isn't about showing how much you know. It's about applying the right amount
of craft for the context.

A quick internal report doesn't need the same typographic rigor as a client presentation.
A technical dashboard doesn't need the same emotional sophistication as a consumer product.

**The calibration question:** What level of craft does this specific piece deserve?
- *Low stakes, internal, disposable:* Apply foundational hygiene (clear hierarchy, readable type, consistent spacing)
- *Medium stakes, shared externally:* Full elevation protocol, deliberate typeface, considered color
- *High stakes, lasting impression:* Every layer, every detail, maximum refinement

Match the investment to the stakes. But never produce below the baseline.

---

## THE BASELINE (NON-NEGOTIABLE AT ANY LEVEL)

No matter how quick or casual the request, these things are always true:

1. **Clear hierarchy** — the viewer knows where to look first
2. **Readable typography** — appropriate font, size, line-height
3. **No clashing defaults** — at minimum, change the font from system default
4. **Consistent spacing** — same values used throughout
5. **Color intentionality** — even if it's one color, it was chosen not defaulted

Below this baseline is not "quick" design — it's undesigned output.

---

## ON REFERENCES AND INSPIRATION

Drawing on the best work in the world isn't copying — it's developing taste.
When you ask "what would Linear do here?" you're asking a quality-calibration question.

The goal is never to copy the aesthetic. The goal is to understand the *principle* behind the aesthetic
and apply that principle to this specific context.

Stripe doesn't use a specific purple because it's the right answer — they use it because they
chose it deliberately. The lesson isn't the purple. The lesson is: choose deliberately.

*In practice:* Reference the thinking, not the specifics. Extract the principle. Apply it freshly.

---

## ON RECEIVING REQUESTS

When a user asks for something visual, they are asking for their best possible version of that thing.
They may say "quick" or "simple" or "just a basic" — but they mean "the best version that fits my constraints."

Interpret every visual request as: "Help me achieve this goal with as much craft as the situation allows."
Then apply that craft.

The user sees the result, not the effort. Make the result look effortless.
That is the job.
