# Reference Library

Design exemplars, principles, and aesthetic systems to draw from.
When you need visual direction, find the most relevant reference and ask: "what would this approach demand here?"

---

## COMPANY / PRODUCT EXEMPLARS

### Stripe
**What they do:** Obsessive typography, deep purple palette with controlled use of gradient,
editorial-quality documentation, charts that feel designed not generated.
**Signature moves:** Floating cards on gradient backgrounds, tabular numbers in clean sans,
generous padding in data tables, subtle purple tints in neutral grays.
**Typographic voice:** Inter or custom Söhne, tightly tracked large numerals.
**When to reference:** Financial dashboards, SaaS product UIs, developer tools, any B2B product.
**Principle to extract:** Every element feels considered. Nothing looks like a default.

### Linear
**What they do:** Dark-first design, extreme restraint, almost no color except for semantic use,
engineering precision in every pixel.
**Signature moves:** Near-black backgrounds (not pure black), purple/violet single-accent,
monospaced labels, hairline borders, no drop shadows.
**Typographic voice:** Inter, tight tracking, medium weight for almost everything.
**When to reference:** Technical tools, project management, developer products, dark UIs.
**Principle to extract:** Restraint as luxury. The absence of decoration is the design.

### Apple (Human Interface Guidelines era)
**What they do:** Spatial hierarchy using blur and translucency, clear navigation structures,
SF Pro typeface with optical sizing, deference to content.
**Signature moves:** Translucent backgrounds, large accessible tap targets, clear/filled icon pairing,
nested rounded rectangles matching device radius.
**Typographic voice:** Large bold headlines, moderate body weight, system fonts.
**When to reference:** Consumer-facing tools, mobile-first presentations, health/wellness dashboards.
**Principle to extract:** The interface should feel physical and obvious. The user should never wonder.

### Bloomberg Terminal / Financial Data Design
**What they do:** Dense information display where every pixel earns its place,
clear color semantics (green/red for up/down, amber for warnings), monospaced data.
**Signature moves:** Tabular numerals right-aligned, high data density with rhythm,
high-contrast UI, orange as anchor color.
**Typographic voice:** Monospaced or condensed sans for data, large for key figures.
**When to reference:** Financial reports, trading tools, operational dashboards, data-dense layouts.
**Principle to extract:** Clarity at density. Information hierarchy that survives aggressive compression.

### Notion
**What they do:** Document-first design, extreme readability, minimal chrome,
collaborative and approachable without being childish.
**Signature moves:** White backgrounds with generous margins, single-weight icon system,
light gray secondary text, emoji as visual anchors.
**Typographic voice:** System fonts, modest size scale, heavy use of whitespace.
**When to reference:** Internal tools, knowledge bases, simple reports, everyday documents.
**Principle to extract:** Get out of the way of the content. The design serves reading, not itself.

### Figma (product/marketing)
**What they do:** Vibrant but controlled color, community-forward aesthetic,
high energy without chaos, components that feel systematic.
**Signature moves:** Bold gradients in marketing, neutral product UI, purple and black as anchors,
rounded corners throughout.
**Typographic voice:** Inter, larger-than-usual headings, bold hierarchy.
**When to reference:** Creative tools, marketing materials, product announcements.
**Principle to extract:** Systems thinking made visible. Everything is a component.

### The Economist
**What they do:** Dense editorial typography, red and black as the sole color system,
charts that are fully explained by their labels, journalistic confidence.
**Signature moves:** Red rule at top of charts, Oxford blue for data, no chart junk,
serif headlines, all-caps rubrics.
**Typographic voice:** Aldus/Utopia serif, small but precise.
**When to reference:** Reports, analysis documents, policy presentations.
**Principle to extract:** Confidence through restraint. Red used once means more than red used ten times.

### Editorial Presentation (Chinese Knowledge Video PPT Style)
**What they do:** White content cards floating on an off-white textured canvas, extremely sparse
layouts, a single navy blue accent color, and precise typographic hierarchy mixing Chinese and
Latin scripts. Characteristic of high-quality Chinese educational and analytical content creators.
**Signature moves:**
- Canvas background is warm off-white or pale warm gray (`#f5f4f0` range) — never pure white, slight texture implied
- White content cards with prominent box-shadow (`0 8px 32px rgba(0,0,0,0.10)`), evoking physical paper suspended above a surface
- Single accent color: mid-deep navy (`#3454a8`), used only for key numbers, italicized emphasis words, and left-side quote bars
- Small all-caps monospaced labels (`CHAPTER 02 · TAKEAWAY`, `SURFACE`, `REAL BUSINESS`) as chapter and column markers
- Left-side vertical bar (3–4px solid navy) marks pull quotes and core arguments — high visual weight
- Two-column layout: left side holds numbered navigation list (large numeral + primary heading + small English subtitle), right side holds main content
- Key terms in body copy set in blue italic, creating precise contrast against black body text
- Hairline dividers (1px light gray) replace color-block section breaks
- Hero statistics in bold italic serif for maximum visual impact (e.g. `$30B`, `8×`)
**Typographic voice:** Sans-serif for headings and navigation; serif italic for oversized data display and in-line English emphasis within CJK body text.
**When to reference:** Knowledge presentations, analytical reports, editorial content cards, any slide deck requiring "scholarly rigor with design conviction."
**Principle to extract:** Spaciousness is a statement, not an omission. Card shadows create physicality. Navy appears only where it matters most.

## DESIGN MOVEMENT REFERENCES

### Swiss International Typographic Style (1950s–present)
**Core principles:** Grid discipline, sans-serif typography, flush-left text,
objective photography, mathematical proportions.
**What it looks like:** Akzidenz-Grotesk or Helvetica, asymmetric layouts with rigid grid,
primary colors + black + white, photography as information not decoration.
**Famous practitioners:** Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann, Emil Ruder.
**When to reference:** Formal reports, corporate communications, any context demanding authority.
**Design demand:** "Is there a grid? Does everything sit on it? Is the typography doing the work?"

### Bauhaus (1919–1933)
**Core principles:** Form follows function, unity of art and craft, geometric reduction,
primary color system (red/yellow/blue/black/white).
**What it looks like:** Geometric shapes as building blocks, no decoration that doesn't serve function,
primary-color hierarchy, industrial precision.
**When to reference:** Instructional materials, infographics, systems that explain complex ideas.
**Design demand:** "Can every element defend its existence? What would happen if I removed it?"

### Japanese Design (Ma principle)
**Core principles:** Ma (間) — negative space as active element, not empty.
Wabi-sabi — beauty of imperfection and impermanence. Simplicity as sophistication.
**What it looks like:** Extreme whitespace, restrained palette, natural textures,
asymmetric balance, seasonal color influences.
**When to reference:** Premium product presentations, luxury branding, minimal digital experiences.
**Design demand:** "Is the empty space doing work? Does the composition feel balanced through absence?"

### New York Times Data Journalism (2010s–present)
**Core principles:** Annotation as design, chart as argument, story in the data.
**What it looks like:** Simple chart forms with rich annotation, color used for narrative not decoration,
serif body with sans data labels, extensive whitespace.
**When to reference:** Data-heavy reports, presentations with charts, analytical dashboards.
**Design demand:** "Does each chart have a thesis? Is the most important insight visually dominant?"

## TYPOGRAPHIC SYSTEMS

### The Classical Scale (Modular)
Typographic scale based on musical ratios: 8, 10, 12, 16, 21, 27, 36, 48, 64px.
Relationships feel harmonious because they're mathematically related.
*Use when building a typographic scale from scratch.*

### The Editorial Scale (Print-derived)
Caption 10, Body 14, Subhead 18, Headline 28, Display 48 — flatter than classical but readable.
Used by newspapers and magazines. Optimized for reading, not hierarchy performance.
*Use when the document will be read at length, not scanned.*

### The Dashboard Scale (Data-derived)
Label 11 (caps), Caption 12, Body 14, Subhead 16, Stat 32, Hero 64.
Compressed in the middle, dramatic at the top. Designed for scanning.
*Use when information needs to be consumed at a glance.*

## COLOR SYSTEMS

### Monochromatic + Single Accent
One neutral family (typically warm or cool gray) + one pure accent. Clean, focused, professional.
Best for: corporate, technical, analytical.
*Exemplars: Linear, early Google, most Japanese product design.*

### Analogous Editorial
Two adjacent hues on the color wheel (e.g., teal + blue, or amber + orange) + neutral.
Creates warmth and cohesion without competing colors.
Best for: creative industries, healthcare, education.
*Exemplars: Mailchimp, Notion docs, many publisher brands.*

### Split Primary
Uses two primary colors in strict ratio (60/10) + neutral. Bold but systematic.
Best for: product marketing, presentations that need energy.
*Exemplars: Stripe (indigo + mint), Figma (purple + black).*

### Financial Semantic Palette
Dark navy/slate as background family, white as primary text, green/red strictly for
directional data, amber for warnings, blue for information. No decorative color.
Best for: financial tools, operational dashboards, any data-dense environment.
*Exemplars: Bloomberg, Robinhood, most fintech.*

## PRINCIPLES FROM PRACTITIONERS

**"Design is not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works."**
— Steve Jobs. *Apply: Every element must serve a function. Decoration that doesn't communicate is noise.*

**"Less, but better."**
— Dieter Rams. *Apply: Before adding, ask what can be removed. The final output should feel inevitable.*

**"I have made this longer than usual because I have not had time to make it shorter."**
— Blaise Pascal. *Apply: Refinement means reduction. First drafts are too busy. Edit visually.*

**"Whitespace is like air: it is necessary for design to breathe."**
— Jan Tschichold. *Apply: Increase all margins and padding beyond first instinct.*

**"The details are not the details. They make the design."**
— Charles Eames. *Apply: Spend disproportionate time on border-radius, spacing increments, color values.*

**"Typography is what language looks like."**
— Ellen Lupton. *Apply: Font choice communicates before a single word is read.*

**"Complexity is not the same as depth. Most things can be simplified without loss."**
— Edward Tufte. *Apply: Remove chart junk. Remove decorative grid lines. Remove legends if you can annotate instead.*
